One trivial event stream, served five ways — short poll, adaptive poll, long poll, Server-Sent Events, WebSocket — measured end-to-end with replicates across client count, event rate, and five network-impairment profiles. The headline is not which transport is fastest. It is how each one fails: push transports lose events, polling only gets staler, and a bad network erases most of the difference between them.

correction · supersedes the single-run mini-sweep

An earlier version of this page reported single-run measurements. Replication refuted three of its headline claims: WebSocket does not beat SSE by 25× on tails (their medians are identical), long poll did not lose 3.8% of events at 2000 clients (it lost none), and adaptive polling's p99 there was 47 ms, not 470 ms. Those figures were inflated 3–35× by a machine that had not fully settled. Every number below is a median across replicates with its spread shown. The retraction stays visible on purpose: it is the strongest evidence that the instrument now works.

findings

What the replicated data says

  • Failure mode, not speed, is what separates these transports. At 10,000 clients fixed polling still delivers 99.9% of events at 47.6% CPU, while SSE drops 17.2%, WebSocket 14.6%, and long poll 78.9%. Polling's load is bounded by the clock, so overload makes it staler; push transports must deliver every event to every client, so overload makes them lose data. Graceful degradation is polling's real advantage, and it is invisible until you push past the knee.
  • SSE and WebSocket are tied on speed and separated by variance. Median p99 is identical at 50, 500, and 2000 clients (4.7/5.8 ms, 10.75 ms, 17.15 ms) and identical under every network profile. What differs is reproducibility: WebSocket's spread is 1.07–1.54× where SSE's is 3.16–11.55× at 20 ev/s — though at 100 ev/s the ordering reverses. Neither is uniformly steadier, and a single-run benchmark would confidently report whichever regime it happened to sample.
  • Impairment compresses the differences. On a clean link the transports span 60× in p50 (504.8 ms vs 8.4 ms); under satellite conditions they span 2.5× (964.7 vs 378.6). Once round-trip time dominates sampling delay, the protocol stops mattering. The practical inversion: transport choice pays off most on good networks, least on bad ones — the opposite of the usual instinct to reach for WebSocket because the network is poor.
  • Fixed polling never gets slower, only staler. Its p99 was 1036.633 ms in every run at every client count up to 5000 — identical to three decimals, exactly the analytic sampling-delay model (p50 ≈ interval⁄2). It is also the cheapest server-side option at every rate tested.
  • “Polling wastes bandwidth” is false on a busy stream. At 100 ev/s fixed poll moved 599 KB/client — essentially identical to WebSocket's 598 KB — because one poll per second batches ~100 events and the per-response header cost amortizes away. Polling is wasteful on quiet streams, where it pays full HTTP overhead for empty answers. That inverts the usual telling.
  • Long poll's cost is proportional to rate × clients. It delivers one event per round trip by construction (measured: exactly 1.00 events/response), so CPU climbs 4.8% → 37.9% → 94.7% as the rate goes 1 → 20 → 100 ev/s. Cheapest transport on a quiet stream; the first to collapse on a busy one, and the worst at scale by a wide margin.
  • Adaptive polling is dominated at every rate and every scale. At 1 ev/s it is worst-of-both: 3.7× fixed poll's bytes and 2.3× its CPU while still 835 ms stale. At scale its floor turns N busy clients into N⁄floor requests per second — a self-inflicted DDoS that reaches a 15.5 s p99 at 5000 clients. No regime in this study favours it.
  • The decision rule. Below the knee and freshness matters → SSE or WebSocket, chosen on API shape and tail predictability. Above the knee, or when losing events is unacceptable → fixed polling, which trades staleness for completeness. Quiet stream, no persistent connections available → long poll. On a genuinely bad network → pick on operational grounds, because the latency difference has mostly evaporated. Adaptive polling → never, at these rates.
instrument log

What the harness caught before publishing

Every entry was found by the apparatus questioning its own output — through validation runs, an adversarial audit, or replication. Four plausible-sounding explanations were tested and refuted, and are recorded so they stay out of the findings.

retracted
Three headline claims, refuted by replication
“WS beats SSE 25×,” “long poll loses 3.8% of events,” and “adaptive poll collapses to 470 ms” all came from single runs on a machine still settling. Replicated: medians identical, zero loss, 47 ms.
fixed
Clock skew censored the fastest 31.8% of an SSE run
Per-process calibration error (~0.3–1.5 ms) made sub-millisecond latencies negative and the histogram silently dropped them — biasing SSE against itself. Now: median-of-301 calibration against the shared system clock, plus clamp-and-count. The ~0.5 ms residual is the stated resolution floor.
fixed
Nagle's algorithm silently favoured WebSocket
The ws library sets TCP_NODELAY internally; raw Node HTTP sockets do not. SSE tails sat at 40 ms delayed-ACK multiples until setNoDelay was applied to every socket → p95 fell from 47 ms to 2.8 ms.
fixed
Two orphaned producers poisoned the impairment rig
Cleanup patterns matched absolute paths while producers are spawned with a relative one, so strays survived — one for over three hours. Two producers on a shared channel, each numbering from zero, corrupted the server's ring buffer and produced 96-second “latencies” and delivery ratios of 15–22×. Fixed by giving every impairment run its own container and its own channel, matching the isolation the main harness already had. That isolation is why the 161 main runs were never at risk.
fixed
An orphaned server served a benchmark cell
A killed sweep left a server alive; a later cell bound to its port and measured the wrong process (confirmed via lsof). Every run now gets a bind-probed port and asserts the responder's instance ID matches the run it launched.
fixed
Poll cursor manufactured phantom loss
Advancing to the server's tail skipped batch-capped backlog and double-charged it as “missed.” Regression test: batch cap 10 at 30 ev/s now yields delivery ratio exactly ⅓ with zero false loss — starvation correctly distinguished from drop.
limit
C10K, hit on the client side
A 10000-client cell failed to connect although the identical scenario had succeeded standalone minutes earlier. Each run leaves ~10k sockets in TIME_WAIT (~30 s on macOS, not tunable without sudo), consuming the ~49k ephemeral port range. Ramp, connect budget, and cooldown now scale with fleet size — but WebSocket at 10000 still fails to establish intermittently, which is why its cell carries a 7.5× spread.
gap
The resilience axis was never exercised
Zero reconnects and zero missed events in all 50 impairment runs, including 3% correlated loss — TCP absorbed everything. That is a real result about these impairment levels, not evidence of transport robustness. Hard link cuts (the flapping profile) are what would test reconnect and resync, and have not been run.
refuted
Ruled out with evidence
permessage-deflate (off by default in ws 8), GC pauses (traced), heartbeat-burst as the stall cause (failed replication under rank-sum), and SSE frame-rebuild unfairness (allocation profile symmetric where it matters).

methodology

How to read these numbers

  • Apples-to-apples by construction. One producer stamps every event with a sequence number and a high-resolution timestamp; all five transports carry the same stream; latency = receive − emit per event, loss = sequence gaps — computed identically for every transport.
  • Median, not mean. A single contaminated run shifts a mean and barely moves a median. Every figure is the median across replicates, with the observed min–max alongside.
  • Replicates are interleaved, not blocked. Each round runs every cell once before repeating, rotating order — so drift and background noise land on all transports equally instead of confounding one.
  • Resolution floor ~0.5 ms. Cross-process timestamps are calibrated against the shared system clock. Differences smaller than that are below the instrument's resolution and are not claims.
  • Impairment is egress-only. netem shapes server→client, so a polling transport's request leg stays fast: any polling disadvantage measured under impairment is a lower bound. A container's virtual NIC also adds ~0.1–0.5 ms, which is why the clean profile is measured in-container as the baseline rather than compared against host loopback.
  • Closed-loop load. Clients wait for a response before re-requesting, so overload appears as latency and self-throttling rather than unbounded queueing. An open-loop fleet would do more damage — read the collapse figures as lower bounds.
  • Single instance, single host. Redis fan-out means K servers could run; every number here is one process on one machine, and the load generator shares that machine. Horizontal scaling is demonstrated as a design property, not measured.

What would finish this

01The resilience axis — hard link cuts rather than loss and delay, to exercise reconnect, resync-from-last-sequence, and missed-update accounting. It is the one dimension the current impairment matrix leaves untouched.
02A second machine — moving the load generator off the server's host removes the client-side port ceiling that currently caps the scale tier, and would let the 10k cells reach the stability bar the rest of the data clears.
03The write-up — the frontier, the measured crossovers, and the variance result, which is the contribution a single-run study structurally cannot produce.
